What a certificate of insurance is

A certificate of insurance is a one-page summary of the insurance a business carries: which policies exist, with which carriers, at what limits, effective which dates. The party asking for it (a general contractor, a property manager, a town office) is called the certificate holder, and what they want is simple: evidence, before your crew walks on the job, that a real policy stands behind you.

Three facts keep the whole subject straight. First, the certificate is issued by the agent as evidence that a policy exists; it is informational, not a contract of its own. Second, it is free: once your policy exists, a standard certificate is routine service, with nothing to pay us or anyone else. Third, it is only as real as the policy behind it. A certificate without a policy is not a head start; it is a forged document, and the red flags section below says so plainly.

Most certificate requests trace back to two policies: workers compensation and general liability. GCs on Long Island routinely require evidence of both before anyone is scheduled.

What a certificate is not

Most certificate disputes on a job site come from expecting the paper to do one of these four things. It does none of them.

Not a policy change

It does not change a policy

A certificate reports what the policy says on the day the certificate is issued. If a GC wants a term the policy does not include, the fix is an endorsement from the carrier, never a wording change on the certificate.

Not protection

It is not itself protection

The policy is what responds to a claim. The certificate is paper about the policy. Handing one over adds nothing to what the policy would actually do, and a certificate can never make up for a policy that is missing something.

Not the same thing

Certificate holder is not additional insured

A certificate holder listing means the GC receives the certificate. Additional insured status exists only as an endorsement on the policy itself, and only the carrier can add one. Confusing the two is the classic job-site mistake.

Not negotiable

It is not a negotiation document

The certificate must mirror the policy, so the time to raise a contract's insurance requirements is before the policy is placed, when the policy can be built to match. Bring us the contract language; that is the working surface.

Additional insured, honestly

The most common demand in a GC contract is "name us as additional insured." What that means: an endorsement written onto your policy that extends certain protection under your policy to the GC, typically for liability arising out of your work for them. It is a real change to the policy, which is exactly why a certificate cannot create it.

The honest mechanics, with no varnish: additional insured status is an endorsement on the policy itself; adding it is the carrier's call, not ours and not the GC's; and under some policies' terms it carries an added premium. We can request it, and on common contractor policies the request is often a routine one, but nobody who is being straight with you will promise a carrier's answer before the carrier gives it.

The practical move: send us the insurance requirements page of the contract before the policy is placed. Requirements that reach us early become part of the application; requirements that surface after issuance become endorsement requests with the carrier deciding on its own clock.

The waiver of subrogation line

Subrogation is the carrier's right, after it pays a claim, to recover its payment from a third party whose fault contributed to the loss. A waiver of subrogation endorsement gives up that recovery right against a named party, almost always the general contractor. That is why GC contracts demand the waiver line on your certificate: the GC does not want your carrier pursuing them after an injury on their site.

The same three honest rules apply. The waiver exists only if the policy actually carries the endorsement. Adding it is the carrier's decision under the policy's terms. And under some policies' terms it carries an added premium. The line on the certificate can only report an endorsement that exists; a certificate that shows a waiver the policy does not contain is exactly the forged-paper problem this page keeps warning about.

The honest calendar

A brand-new business with no prior policy is the fast case, and the next section covers it. An established shop switching carriers typically waits 5 to 25 days, and most of that wait is loss runs (typically five years) that only the incumbent carrier can produce, so a switch should be planned around a renewal date, not a GC deadline. And if your deadline is tomorrow but you already carry an active policy through another office, call that office today: a standard certificate from an active policy is a same-business-day request almost everywhere.

Why speed is realistic for a newer shop

A shop in roughly its first eighteen months, with no prior workers comp history, has the fastest honest door in commercial insurance: e-application, e-signature, electronic premium deposit; coverage can take effect 12:01 a.m. the following day, if the carrier approves. Everything moves electronically, and there is no incumbent carrier whose records have to be collected first.

That is not a promise, and it is not the same as walking out with paper in hand: the carrier approves the application or it does not, on its own review. But it means a certificate demand with a one-day fuse is sometimes survivable for a brand-new business, provided the application goes in immediately and the carrier says yes. The certificate is issued after the policy exists, never before. The full walkthrough for a first-time employer is in workers comp for the new contractor.

Red flags, said plainly

Deadline pressure is when bad actors show up. Two things are simply crimes, and one pattern should end a conversation.

Backdating is fraud. If anyone suggests dating a policy, an application, or a certificate earlier than the day it actually took effect, that is insurance fraud, not a favor. It does not fix the gap; it converts a paperwork problem into a criminal one, for you as well as for whoever signs it. We do not backdate anything, for any job, ever.
A certificate for a policy that does not exist is a crime. A COI is evidence that a policy exists. Producing one without the policy behind it is creating a forged insurance document, and presenting one to a GC is passing that forgery along. Contractors get caught at exactly the worst moment: after an injury, when the certificate is the first thing everyone pulls.

The pattern that should end a conversation: anyone offering a certificate within the hour with no application, no e-signature, and no named carrier. A real certificate has a real policy behind it, and a real policy has a carrier that made a decision. Anyone selling you the paper without the policy is selling you the evidence for a fraud case with your business name on it.

If you are behind on this and the deadline is real, call (631) 452-4075 and say so. The honest path is faster than most people fear, and it is the only one that survives an audit, a claim, or a lawyer reading the file later.

Certificate questions, answered

The short versions. Everything beyond certificates lives in the full questions and answers.

What does a certificate of insurance cost?

Nothing. Once your policy exists, a standard certificate is routine service from the office that placed the policy, and it is free. If anyone quotes you a price for the certificate itself, treat that as a warning sign about the whole operation.

Premium is a separate matter entirely, and premium is always paid to the insurance carrier directly, never to us.

Can I get a COI today if I do not have a policy yet?

No, and be suspicious of anyone who says yes. A certificate summarizes a policy that already exists; a certificate without a policy behind it is a forged document, not a head start.

What a brand-new business can honestly do is still fast: e-application, e-signature, electronic premium deposit; coverage can take effect 12:01 a.m. the following day, if the carrier approves. The certificate follows the policy, never the other way around. The full path is in workers comp for the new contractor.

Is being the certificate holder the same as being an additional insured?

No, and the two are confused on job sites every day. The certificate holder is simply the party the certificate is addressed to; the listing itself grants them nothing under the policy.

Additional insured status exists only as an endorsement written onto the policy itself. Adding it is the carrier's decision, and under some policies' terms it carries an added premium. If a contract requires additional insured status, tell us before the policy is placed so the request goes to the carrier up front.

What is a waiver of subrogation, and why does my GC demand it?

Subrogation is the carrier's right, after it pays a claim, to recover from a third party whose fault contributed to the loss. A waiver of subrogation endorsement gives up that recovery right against a named party, usually the general contractor, which is why GC contracts demand it: they do not want your carrier coming after them later.

The honest mechanics are the same as additional insured: the waiver exists only if the policy carries the endorsement, adding it is the carrier's decision, and under some policies' terms it carries an added premium. The line on the certificate can only report an endorsement that actually exists.

My GC says the certificate wording is wrong. Can you just fix it?

Only by fixing the policy. A certificate must mirror what the policy actually says; editing the certificate to say something the policy does not is exactly the kind of document nobody should want their name on.

If the contract requires a term the policy does not include, the real fix is an endorsement request to the carrier, and the carrier decides. Send us the contract's insurance requirements and we will read them against the policy and tell you plainly what is already there, what can be requested, and what the carrier would have to approve.

Can you backdate a certificate or a policy so it looks like I had insurance last week?

No. Backdating an insurance document is fraud, full stop, and we will not do it for any job, any deadline, or any amount of pleading. Anyone who offers to is telling you exactly how they would treat your claim someday.

The honest fast path exists and it is real: a brand-new business can e-apply, e-sign, and pay a premium deposit electronically, and coverage can take effect 12:01 a.m. the following day, if the carrier approves. Start today and tomorrow can be day one. Yesterday is not available to anyone.
