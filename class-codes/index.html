What a class code actually is

Every kind of work in New York workers compensation is assigned a four-digit classification code with an official description, called the phraseology. Each class carries a published loss cost: a rate per $100 of payroll, filed by the New York Compensation Insurance Rating Board, that reflects how often and how badly people doing that work get hurt statewide. Plumbing (class 5183) carries a different number than electrical wiring (5190), which carries a different number than excavation (6217). Your code is the first input of your entire workers comp calculation, before payroll, before history, before any carrier factor.

Effective October 1, 2026, NYCIRB R.C. Bulletin 2659 moves the statewide loss cost level down 21.9%, so the number attached to your class is about to change. The three loss costs shown in the lookup below come from that bulletin; every other class shows its phraseology and safety group count only, because we do not publish figures we have not verified against the filing.

Why misclassification matters at audit

Workers comp premium is provisional until the year-end audit. The auditor looks at what the crew actually did, and if payroll sat under a lower-rated class than the work performed, the payroll is typically reallocated to the correct class and the carrier bills the difference for the whole year, after the money is spent. Misclassification in the other direction is quieter and just as expensive: a shop paying a higher-rated code than its work requires overpays every payroll cycle with no refund arriving on its own.

Records decide these arguments. Splitting one crew's payroll across classes generally requires verifiable time records; without them, an auditor can put the payroll in the highest-rated class that applies. One New York rule helps the trades specifically: for eligible construction classes, the payroll used in the calculation is capped at $1,922.25 per employee per week effective July 1, 2026 (NYCIRB Manual Rule V(G)), so a well-paid crew is not rated as if every dollar of wages were exposure.

New York is not an NCCI state

Check where your paperwork comes from. Most states use NCCI classifications, and the internet is full of NCCI code lists. New York is not an NCCI state; it maintains its own classification system through NYCIRB. The NCCI plumbing and electrical codes you may find elsewhere, 5537 and 5551, do not exist in New York. If a quote, a template, or an out-of-state broker put one of those on your New York paperwork, the classification is wrong at the root. The lookup below contains only New York classes.

What the safety group count means

The "safety groups accept this class" line is the most useful number in the lookup, and the easiest to misread. Here is the honest version.

The lever

Published upfront discount

NYSIF safety groups publish an average upfront discount of 32.5%, applied from the start of the policy. The published figures are on the NYSIF safety group page.

The look-back

Historical dividends

Groups have historically declared annual dividends in the 20 to 30 percent range, based on each group's actual results. History is all it is: dividends are declared yearly and are never assured, and treating one as a sure thing is the classic safety-group mistake.

The gate

Admission is decided, not automatic

A group listing your class means the door exists, not that it is open. The group manager reviews the shop, NYSIF underwriting approves it, and a clean safety record does most of the talking. Transfers from an existing NYSIF policy happen at renewal.

Reading the count

More groups, more paths

A class listed by ten groups has ten possible doors and real competition among administrators. A class listed by one group has exactly one path to safety-group pricing. Either way the count tells you who to talk to before renewal, which is a conversation we have for free.

Where this fits the bigger picture: class codes drive the baseline, safety groups shape the discount, and the workers comp page walks the whole chain, including the October 2026 loss cost drop and how fast a policy can start. We raise the safety-group option where the research supports it; we are not the decision-maker, and we do not speak for NYSIF.
