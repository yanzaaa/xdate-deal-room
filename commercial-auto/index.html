Why a personal auto policy is usually the wrong tool

A personal auto policy is priced for commuting and errands. Most personal policies sold in New York contain business-use exclusions or limitations, and a van with ladder racks, a wrapped logo, and a bed full of copper is not a hard call for a claims adjuster whose job is to establish how the vehicle was being used at the time of the loss.

Here is the honest version: we have not read your policy, and we will not pretend to. Whether any specific personal policy responds to any specific loss is a question only that policy's own language and its carrier can answer. What we can say is that this question tends to get asked after the crash, at the worst possible moment, and that the way to make it a boring question is to put a revenue-earning vehicle on a policy written for revenue-earning vehicles.

The stakes are not just the truck. A serious at-fault crash is a liability event, and liability follows the business. If a general contractor is already asking you for proof of insurance before you get on the job, start with general liability; the vehicle question usually comes up in the same conversation.

We never claim to know what your current policy says. Everything on this page is probability language about how most policies in the market are written, not a statement about yours. If you want the question settled for your shop, that is a fifteen-minute call: (631) 452-4075.

What commercial auto handles

Three jobs, one policy. Each part exists because a personal policy was never designed to do it for a business.

The big one

Liability

Bodily injury and property damage your vehicle causes to other people. This is the part that protects the business itself when a crash turns into a claim or a lawsuit, and it is the reason commercial limits are sized for a business, not a commute.

The truck itself

Physical damage

Collision and comprehensive for your own vehicles: the crash, the tree limb, the theft from the yard. If a truck out of service means a crew out of work, this is the part that gets you back on the road.

Often missed

Hired and non-owned autos

An employee runs to the supply house in their own car, or you rent a box truck for a week. The business can still be named in a suit arising from that trip. Hired and non-owned auto liability is the piece built for exactly that exposure.

The third box matters: a crew member hurt in a crash while working is generally a workers compensation question, and New York requires that policy from your first employee under the NY Workers Compensation Law.

Trailers, and the tools riding in the back

A trailer usually raises two separate questions. Liability arising from the rig while it is hitched to an insured power unit is commonly addressed through the policy on the power unit, while physical damage to the trailer itself typically needs the trailer scheduled on the policy. How any specific policy treats a specific trailer depends on the form the carrier issues, so we treat it as a question to settle in writing during the application, never an assumption.

The tools are the part we flag hardest, because it surprises people. The compressor, the pipe threader, and the hand tools in the back are usually not an auto question at all. Most commercial auto forms are about the vehicle; property being transported is typically the territory of a commercial property or inland marine policy. A shop can have the truck insured and the contents of the truck addressed nowhere, and not find out until the morning the van is empty.

Honest flag: this page cannot tell you whether your tools are protected today, and neither can we until the policies are on the table. If tool and equipment protection matters to your shop, say so on the application or on the phone and we will put the inland marine question in front of the carrier alongside the auto application.

What shapes the price

No dollar figures here, because none would be honest. We never state a premium without a completed application. These are the four inputs that carry the most weight in a carrier's review.

Radius of operation

Where the trucks go. A fleet that works within Nassau and Suffolk presents differently to an underwriter than one running upstate or out of state every week. More miles in unfamiliar territory means more exposure.

Who drives

The driving records of the people behind the wheel. Carriers review listed drivers, which is why we ask for the driver list up front rather than letting it surface as a surprise later in the process.

Vehicle type and use

A half-ton pickup, a box truck, and a dump truck are different exposures. Weight, value, and what the vehicle actually does all feed the carrier's math.

Claims history

Prior losses follow the business. Clean years help. A past loss does not end the conversation, it just belongs in the conversation honestly, and up front.

Quick answers

Four questions we hear most on this line. The full FAQ goes deeper.

The truck is titled in my personal name. Is that a problem?

Titling is one input; use is the bigger one. Most carriers ask how a vehicle earns its keep, not just whose name is on the title, and a vehicle that works in the trade generally belongs on a commercial policy regardless of how it is titled. Bring the titling up on the call and you will get a straight answer for your situation, not a generic one.

Will my general liability policy respond if my driver causes a crash?

We cannot say what any specific policy will do, because we have not read it. What we can say is that most general liability forms treat the operation of autos as a separate subject and point it at an auto policy. That division of labor is why the two policies exist side by side. The general liability page explains what GL is actually for, and this comparison walks through which policy answers which loss.

Are the tools in my van insured under commercial auto?

Usually that is not an auto question. Most commercial auto forms address the vehicle, and property being transported is typically a commercial property or inland marine subject. Whether your tools are protected today depends on policies we have not seen, so we treat it as an open question until the paperwork answers it. Raise it during the application and it gets put to the carrier directly.

My employees sometimes drive their own cars for work. Does that affect me?

It can. A business can be named in a suit arising from an employee's work errand even though the business does not own the car; hired and non-owned auto liability exists for that exposure. And if the employee is hurt in that crash while working, that is generally a workers compensation question, which New York requires you to have answered from your first employee.
