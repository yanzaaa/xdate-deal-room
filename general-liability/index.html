What general liability responds to

Three claim families, all with the same shape: someone outside your company says your work caused them harm. A general liability policy is built to respond to the claim and, usually the bigger number, the legal defense.

People

Third-party bodily injury

A customer trips over your cord. A passerby is struck by material coming off a ladder. The injured person is not your employee, and the claim lands on your general liability policy, defense costs included.

Property

Third-party property damage

A torch scorches a finished cabinet run. A fitting lets go and floods the unit below. Damage to property that belongs to someone else is the second core job of the policy.

After the job

Completed operations

The work is done, you are paid, you are gone, and months later something you installed fails and causes damage. Completed operations is the part of GL built for claims that arrive after the work is finished.

What it is not built for

The most expensive GL mistake is expecting it to do another line's job. Three things it does not respond to, and where each one actually belongs.

Workers comp

Your crew's injuries

An injury to your own employee belongs to workers compensation, which New York requires from the first employee. GL policies are written to exclude employee injury precisely because comp exists.

Workers comp for contractors →
Tools and gear

Your own tools and equipment

Tools stolen from the van, a compressor damaged on site: that is your property, so GL does not respond. Protecting owned equipment is inland marine and property territory, worth raising in the same conversation.

Commercial auto

Your truck

A vehicle used in the trade belongs on a commercial auto policy. GL generally steps aside the moment a claim involves operating a vehicle, which is exactly when the numbers get large.

Why GCs and property owners ask for it

A general contractor or building owner who lets you on site is exposed to your work. Their fix is contractual, and it arrives as two requests.

The certificate of insurance. Before you start, the GC's compliance desk asks for a COI: a one-page summary showing your lines, limits, and policy dates. It is proof, not a policy, and the requests tend to arrive the week the job starts, which is the worst possible week to be starting an application from zero.

Additional insured status. Most job contracts also require the GC or owner to be added to your policy as an additional insured, so that for claims arising from your work, the policy is designed to respond for them too. That takes an endorsement, and the exact wording the contract asks for matters. A certificate that does not match the contract language gets kicked back, and the job waits while it does.

Three contract items most Long Island subs should review

We work from public data and the documents you share, never from your current policy, so these are items to review, not statements about what any particular policy includes or excludes. Bring your declarations pages to the fifteen-minute call and we go through them line by line.

Item to review

Labor-only subs

If part of your labor is 1099, GCs typically require a certificate from each sub, and carriers commonly look hard at uninsured sub payroll at audit. Collecting a certificate from every sub before they start is a far easier conversation than the one at audit time.

Item to review

Waiver of subrogation

After paying a claim, an insurer can normally pursue whoever caused the loss. GC contracts often ask you to waive that right in their favor, and most standard forms only recognize the waiver by endorsement. If a contract asks for one, confirm it in writing before signing, not after.

Item to review

Per-project aggregate

A GL policy carries a general aggregate, the most it pays across a policy year, and on most standard forms that limit is shared across every job you touch. Larger GCs often require a per-project aggregate endorsement so one bad job elsewhere cannot exhaust the limit on theirs.

These three come up constantly in Nassau and Suffolk job contracts, but every contract reads differently. The habit that saves subs real money is simple: send the insurance exhibit to your producer before you sign, while the wording can still be negotiated. General liability questions, answered plainly

The five we hear most from Nassau and Suffolk shops. Longer answers live on the FAQ page and the blog.

What exactly is a certificate of insurance?

A one-page summary a producer issues showing your lines, limits, and policy dates. It is evidence for the GC, not a policy, and it grants nothing by itself: the endorsements behind it, additional insured wording especially, do the actual work. That is why matching the certificate to the contract language matters more than producing the page quickly.

What does "additional insured" actually mean?

The GC or property owner is added to your policy by endorsement, so that for claims arising from your work, the policy is designed to respond for them as well as for you. Contracts frequently specify exact endorsement wording, and a mismatch is the most common reason a certificate gets kicked back by a compliance desk.

Does general liability respond to my employees’ injuries?

No. Employee injuries belong to workers compensation, which New York requires for virtually every employer from the first employee. GL forms are written to exclude employee injury precisely because comp exists. The full comparison is in general liability vs workers comp.

I am a one-person shop with no employees. Do I still need GL?

New York does not mandate general liability the way it mandates workers compensation, but the market does: most GCs, property managers, and job contracts require a certificate before work starts. For most one-person trades the practical question is not whether a GC will ask, it is whether you want to be applying the same week the job is supposed to begin.
