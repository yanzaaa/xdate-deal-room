Workers compensation rate filings

NY workers comp rates drop on October 1, 2026. Here is the Long Island contractor checklist.

The published baseline that every New York workers comp price is built on falls 21.9% this fall. That does not mean every bill falls 21.9%, and three things on your policy will not move at all unless somebody checks them. Here is the whole picture, with the filing it comes from.

Published August 1, 2026 · 6 minute read · Anthony Yanza, NY licensed producer no. 1986535

What actually changed

The New York Compensation Insurance Rating Board, the body that publishes the rating values behind every workers comp policy in the state, issued Bulletin 2659. Its headline: the statewide workers comp loss cost level drops 21.9% effective October 1, 2026. The change applies by class code, so some trades move more than others, but the direction across the state is down.

This matters to nearly every employer on Long Island because New York requires workers compensation from the first employee for virtually every business. If you have a crew, you have this policy, and the baseline under it just moved.

First, what is a loss cost?

A loss cost is not your price. It is the published claims-cost component per $100 of payroll for a given class code: the rating board's estimate of what injuries in that class actually cost, before any carrier adds its own expenses and profit margin. Each carrier files a multiplier that it applies on top of the loss cost, and then your experience modifier and your payroll estimate shape the final figure.

So the arithmetic every shop should picture is roughly:

loss cost × carrier multiplier × experience mod × payroll

Bulletin 2659 moves only the first term. That is exactly why the change is worth a check rather than a celebration: the other three terms are still whatever they were. We walk through each term with examples in how workers comp pricing actually works in New York.

The class 5183 example: plumbing

Class 5183 is the plumbing classification. Under Bulletin 2659 its published loss cost goes from 4.365 to 3.519 per $100 of payroll, a drop of 19.4%.

To be precise about what this is: a published component of the rate, cited to the filing. It is not a premium, and no one can tell you what your figure will be without a completed application. But when the claims-cost component of a plumbing class falls 19.4%, the baseline for most plumbing shops in that class fell with it.

Why a renewal after October 1 deserves a second look

If your policy renews on or after October 1, 2026, it should be priced against the new loss costs. Most shops in that position will see the change flow through at renewal. The reason to re-check, or fully re-shop, is that the drop is not uniform and not automatic in the ways that matter:

  • The baseline moved, so relative position moved. A carrier whose multiplier looked ordinary against the old loss costs can look expensive against the new ones. The market repriced underneath your policy.
  • Class codes shift by different amounts. The statewide level is down 21.9%, but each class has its own change. Whether your classes moved more or less than the average is a lookup, not a guess.
  • Programs stack on top. For eligible trades, NYSIF safety groups publish upfront discounts averaging 32.5%, with historical annual dividends that have averaged around 30%. Dividends are never assured; they are declared yearly, and admission requires the group manager's acceptance and NYSIF underwriting. We wrote up how these groups work for Long Island trades here.
Renewing before October 1? The new loss costs take effect for policies effective on or after that date. A renewal in August or September is priced on the old baseline, which makes the following renewal, in 2027, the one where this filing shows up. Worth a calendar note, not a panic.

What does not change automatically

Three terms in the pricing arithmetic sit outside Bulletin 2659 entirely. Nobody at the rating board touches them, and left alone they quietly decide whether the loss cost drop reaches you in full.

  • Your carrier's multiplier. Each carrier files its own factor over the loss costs. A filing that lowers loss costs says nothing about what any carrier does with its multiplier, which is why comparing carriers after the change is the whole exercise.
  • Your experience mod. The mod reflects your own claims history against your class's expected losses. It follows your record, not the bulletin, and it multiplies everything.
  • Your payroll estimate. Policies are priced on estimated payroll and settled at audit. An estimate carried forward from a bigger year keeps the whole calculation inflated regardless of what the loss costs do. Your payroll figure always comes from you, never from a guess.

The five-step checklist for Long Island shops

  1. Find your renewal date. Before or after October 1, 2026? That one fact decides whether this filing reaches your next renewal or the one after it.
  2. Pull your declarations page. Note your class codes, your estimated payroll, and your experience mod. Five minutes, and every later step depends on it.
  3. Look up your class changes. The statewide level is down 21.9%; your codes have their own figures. We run this lookup as part of a fifteen-minute check, with the filing cited.
  4. Re-check the mod and the payroll estimate. If headcount or wages dropped since the estimate was set, say so before the renewal is priced. These two terms do not fix themselves.
  5. Get a comparison if you renew after October 1. The baseline moved, so most shops in the affected classes have a reason to see at least one alternative figure next to the incumbent's. If you are brand new with no prior workers comp history, the path is faster than you think: a new employer can e-apply, e-sign, and pay a deposit electronically, and coverage can take effect at 12:01 a.m. the following day if the carrier approves. An established shop switching carriers typically waits 5 to 25 days, mostly on loss runs from the incumbent.
The honest caveat. Everything above is from published filings, and none of it is your number. Individual results depend on your application, your classes, your history, and the carrier's decision, which always belongs to the carrier. We never state a premium without a completed application, and we work from public data in probability language: most shops in a class, never a claim about your policy, which we have not seen.
