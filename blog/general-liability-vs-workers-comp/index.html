General liability vs workers comp: what each one actually does.

The two policies every Long Island contractor gets asked about answer two different questions. General liability answers for harm your work causes to other people and their property. Workers compensation answers for your own crew, and in New York it is the law from the first employee. Here is the whole comparison in five minutes.

Two policies, two different questions

When a GC, a property owner, or an insurance application asks whether you carry "liability and comp," it is really asking two separate questions, and mixing them up is the most common insurance mistake in the trades.

General liability is about everyone who is not on your payroll. The customer who trips over your cord, the ceiling your fitting flooded, the finished cabinet run your torch scorched. When your work harms a third party's body or property, GL is the line that responds to the claim and, usually the bigger number, pays for the legal defense.

Workers compensation is about your own crew. When someone on your payroll is hurt working, comp pays for the medical care and a portion of lost wages through a no-fault system, instead of a lawsuit. And unlike GL, it is not a contractual courtesy: New York requires workers compensation for virtually every employer from the first employee. Not the tenth employee. The first.

Side by side

General liabilityWorkers compensation
Who it protects People outside your company: customers, neighbors, the GC, and their property Your own employees when they are injured on the job
What triggers it A claim that your operations or your finished work injured a third party or damaged their property A work-related injury or illness to someone on your payroll
Who requires it Contracts: GCs, property owners, landlords, and lenders, before you get on the job New York State, by statute, for virtually every employer
Legally required in NY? Generally not by statute for most trades, but near-universal in practice because jobs demand it Yes, from the first employee (NY Workers Compensation Law)

One more New York wrinkle while you are here: statutory short-term disability (DBL) and Paid Family Leave (PFL) are separate mandatory items for private NY employers, apart from workers comp. They live on our employee benefits page, with the state's own overview at wcb.ny.gov.

Three scenarios from one Long Island job

The fastest way to keep the two lines straight is to ask one question at the moment something goes wrong: who was harmed?

Workers comp

A helper falls off a ladder

The person on the ladder is on your payroll, so this is a workers compensation claim. Medical care and lost wages flow through the comp system rather than a lawsuit against you. This exact moment is why New York makes comp mandatory from the first employee.

Workers comp for contractors → General liability

A fitting lets go and floods the client's ceiling

The soaked ceiling belongs to your customer, not to you. Damage to someone else's property is general liability's core job, and the defense costs that come with the claim are usually the larger share of what the policy handles.

General liability, explained →
Both, same afternoon

One accident, two claims

The helper falls and knocks a plank onto the homeowner's car. One accident, two harmed parties: your employee's injury goes to workers comp, the homeowner's car goes to general liability. Needing both lines on the same job is not the exception in the trades. It is the normal case.

Why the GC asks for both on your certificate

Before most commercial or new-construction jobs start, the GC's compliance desk asks for a certificate of insurance, the one-page snapshot of your policies. On Long Island that request almost always names both lines, and the reason is self-interest, not paperwork love. A GC that brings a sub without workers comp onto a job can end up answering for that sub's injuries, and a sub without general liability leaves the owner's property claims with nowhere to land except further up the chain. So the certificate is a gate: both lines shown, or the start date slips while everyone waits.

That is also why the two policies get bundled in conversation even though they answer different questions. The job site needs both because a bad afternoon rarely limits itself to one kind of harm.

The expensive assumption: carrying one and expecting it to do both jobs

We have not read your policy, and we will not pretend to know what is in it. What we can tell you is how the standard forms are usually written, and what that makes likely.

Most general liability policies sold to contractors are written to exclude injuries to the policyholder's own employees, precisely because the law already routes those injuries to workers compensation. And workers comp, for its part, does nothing for the customer's flooded ceiling; it only responds to your own crew. So a shop carrying GL alone most likely has no answer for a payroll injury, and a shop carrying comp alone most likely has no answer for the claim a customer or GC brings. Shops in that position tend to discover the gap on the worst possible day, with a claim already open.

The comp half of that gap is also a legal problem, not just a financial one, because New York requires comp from the first employee and enforces it. If the crew exists, the comp question is already decided for you; the only real choice is where to place it, which is what the pricing system and safety groups are about.

Adding workers comp this year? Timing is on your side. The statewide loss cost level, the published baseline comp prices are built from, drops 21.9% effective October 1, 2026 (NYCIRB Bulletin 2659). The full story is in our rate change post.

A two-minute certificate check

You do not need to read a policy to spot the classic gap. The certificate of insurance shows both lines on one page.

  1. 1

    Pull your current certificate

    Ask whoever placed your insurance for a current certificate of insurance. It is a one-page summary and any producer can issue it the same day.

  2. 2

    Look for both grids

    General liability sits in the top grid, workers compensation below it. Most GC compliance desks on Long Island want to see both before a sub is cleared to start.

  3. 3

    Ask why anything is missing

    If a line is absent, ask why. New York law decides the workers comp question for you from the first employee. The contract you signed usually decides the GL question.
