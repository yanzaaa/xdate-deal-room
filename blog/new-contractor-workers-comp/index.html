Workers comp for new businesses

New contractor on Long Island? Here is how workers comp works when the business is brand new.

The day you hire employee number one, New York requires workers compensation. The good news most new Nassau and Suffolk shops have not heard: a business with no prior comp history can often move faster than an established shop switching carriers, because there is nothing old to collect.

The requirement starts with employee number one

New York does not phase workers comp in as you grow. Under the New York Workers Compensation Law, virtually every employer needs a policy from the first employee, full time or part time. There is no three-employee threshold, no grace period for new businesses, and the Workers Compensation Board runs a public coverage lookup, so the gap is visible to anyone who checks, including the general contractors who hire you.

For a new shop that usually means the requirement arrives before the first invoice does. You win the job, you need a helper, and suddenly you need a policy in place before that helper starts. The full picture of the line, class codes, payroll, audits, safety groups, lives on the workers comp page. This post is about the one question new owners actually ask: how fast can this realistically happen?

Why a brand-new business can move faster than an established one

It sounds backwards, but it is a paperwork fact. The slow part of insuring an established shop is history. A new shop has none.

When an established business switches carriers, the new carrier wants five years of loss runs, the claim history reports held by the incumbent carrier. Requesting, receiving, and reviewing them typically takes 5 to 25 days, and most of that clock belongs to the incumbent, not to you or your producer.

A brand-new business with no prior workers comp history has no loss runs, because there is nothing to run. So the whole process can happen electronically: you e-apply, you e-sign the application yourself, you pay a premium deposit electronically to the carrier, and coverage can take effect at 12:01 a.m. the following day, if the carrier approves the application. That last clause is not fine print. Approval always belongs to the carrier, and nothing on this page or any page is a promise of acceptance.

The practical takeaway: if the crew starts Monday, a new business that applies Friday still has a real path. An established shop that decides to switch on Friday does not. The new-employer speed advantage exists exactly once, at the start, so use it deliberately rather than discovering it in a rush.

What the application actually asks for

The online application is ten fields, and the ones that matter most are the ones only you can supply. We never fill them in for you, and we never guess.

  1. 1

    Your payroll estimate

    Workers comp pricing is built on payroll, so the application asks what you expect to pay your people this year. It comes from you, not from us, and the honest number is the cheap one: policies are audited against actual payroll at year end.

  2. 2

    Your entity type

    Sole proprietor, LLC, or corporation. It changes who is automatically counted, who can seek an exclusion, and what the state expects from you, so the application needs the entity exactly as it is filed, not as you plan to file it.

  3. 3

    Your headcount and duties

    How many people, and what they actually do on the job. Duties drive the class code, and the class code drives the rate the carrier applies to your payroll. Vague answers here become expensive corrections later.

Everything else, the class code research, the published rate context, the carrier paperwork, is work we prepare from public filings and from what you send. If you want to see how those published numbers turn into a price, the walkthrough is in how workers comp pricing works in New York. Two pieces of that context worth knowing on day one: the statewide loss cost level drops 21.9% effective October 1, 2026 per NYCIRB Bulletin 2659, and New York safety groups publish upfront discounts for shops that qualify, with admission decided by the group manager and NYSIF underwriting, never by us.

Three day-one mistakes new shops make

All three are common, all three are avoidable, and all three cost more to fix than to prevent.

Timing

Waiting until the GC asks

Certificate requests arrive the week the job starts, which is the worst week to begin an application from zero. The new-employer path is fast, but it is not instant, and it always depends on carrier approval. Apply when you hire, not when a compliance desk emails you.

Payroll

Guessing payroll low

A low payroll estimate does not save money, it defers a bill. Workers comp policies are audited, and the audit reconciles what you estimated against what you actually paid. Underestimating means a larger catch-up amount at the worst possible time. Estimate honestly and the audit becomes routine.

Paperwork

Counting on a certificate without a policy

A certificate of insurance is evidence that a policy exists, not a substitute for one. No policy, no certificate, and compliance desks verify. The slow part is always issuance, never the certificate, so the order is application first. The full explanation is in general liability and in general liability vs workers comp.

Why you e-sign it yourself, and why that protects you

One detail of the process deserves its own explanation. When your application is ready, you review it and e-sign it personally. A producer may never sign an insurance application for you, and you should walk away from anyone who offers to.

The reason is not ceremony. The application is your statement to the carrier about your business: your payroll, your entity, your people. The carrier prices and approves based on those statements, and you are the only person who actually knows them. Signing it yourself means nothing goes to a carrier in your name that you have not read, and it means the figures on file are yours, not somebody's guess. That is also why premium goes to the carrier directly, never to this office, and why the producer is paid commission by the carrier rather than a fee by you.
