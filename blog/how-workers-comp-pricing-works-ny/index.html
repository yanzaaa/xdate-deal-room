Workers compensation pricing, explained

How workers comp pricing works in New York, from class code to final premium.

Every workers comp price in this state is the same six terms multiplied together. None of them is secret. Here is each one, who controls it, what moves it, and why nobody honest can tell you your number before you complete an application.

Published July 4, 2026 · 8 minute read · Anthony Yanza, NY licensed producer no. 1986535

The formula on one line

New York requires workers compensation from the first employee for virtually every business, so if you run a crew on Long Island, you buy this policy every year. What most shops never see is how the figure on the bill is assembled. The arithmetic is roughly:

loss cost × carrier multiplier × experience mod × payroll (per $100), minus credits and program discounts

Six terms. Two are published for anyone to read. One is filed by each carrier separately, which is the entire reason shopping works. Two come from your own shop. And the last is a set of programs you either qualify for or do not. Let us take them in order.

One scope note before we start: this article is about workers comp only. If you are still sorting out which policy handles which risk, the plain-English split is in general liability vs workers comp.

Term 1: the class code (what your crew actually does)

Every kind of work carries a classification code, because a roofing crew and a bookkeeping office do not produce the same injuries. The code is assigned from the operations of the business, not from what the business calls itself. Plumbing work in New York is class 5183, and a lot of HVAC work lands in the same class alongside it.

The class code matters more than any other term you do not control, because every published rate value hangs off it. It is also the term most often quietly wrong: a shop that changed its mix of work years ago can still be rated on the old description. Most shops have never had anyone read their class codes back to them and ask whether that is still what the crew does all day. It is a five-minute question with a filing-sized consequence.

Term 2: the loss cost (published, and it just moved)

For each class code, the New York Compensation Insurance Rating Board publishes a loss cost: its estimate of what injury claims in that class cost, per $100 of payroll, before any carrier adds its own expenses. This is a public rate component, not a price, and it is filed with the state where anyone can check it.

It is also not standing still. NYCIRB Bulletin 2659 drops the statewide loss cost level 21.9% effective October 1, 2026. For class 5183, the plumbing classification, the published loss cost goes from 4.365 to 3.519 per $100 of payroll, down 19.4%.

Each class moves by its own amount, so the statewide 21.9% is an average, not a promise about your codes. What the change does for your renewal specifically, and what it does not do, is the subject of our October 2026 rate-drop checklist.

Term 3: the carrier multiplier (why the same shop gets different prices)

The loss cost only estimates claims. A carrier also has expenses, taxes, and a margin, so each carrier files its own multiplier, often called a loss cost multiplier or LCM, that it applies on top of the published loss cost. This filing is the carrier's own business decision, made class by class, based on its appetite and its results.

Read that again, because it is the single most useful fact in this article: two carriers pricing the identical shop, same class, same payroll, same history, can lawfully produce different figures, because their filed multipliers differ. That is not haggling and it is not a loyalty reward. It is arithmetic that was filed months before you ever applied. It is also the entire case for comparing the market at renewal instead of assuming the incumbent's multiplier is still competitive, especially in a year when the loss costs underneath every multiplier just moved.

Term 4: your payroll (estimated up front, settled at audit)

Workers comp is priced per $100 of payroll, so your payroll is the volume knob on the whole formula. Here is the part that surprises people: the policy is issued on an estimate. You project the year's payroll when you apply, the carrier prices on that projection, and after the policy year ends the carrier audits the actual figure and the difference settles up, in either direction.

The audit is not an accusation and it is not optional. It is simply the year's estimate being replaced by the year's reality. A shop that grew pays the difference; a shop that shrank gets the difference back. The calm way to live with audits is to estimate honestly, keep clean payroll records by class of work, and update the estimate mid-term if headcount changes materially instead of letting a big true-up pile up. One house rule of ours belongs here: your payroll figure always comes from you. We never estimate it for you, and no honest producer should.

Term 5: the experience mod (your own history, for larger shops)

Once a shop is large enough to qualify for experience rating under the rating board's rules, it gets an experience modifier: a factor built from its own claims history compared with what is expected for its class. Better-than-expected history pushes the factor below the neutral point and multiplies the whole price down; worse history pushes it up. Smaller shops below the eligibility threshold simply do not carry a mod.

The mod is the slowest-moving term, because it looks back over prior years, and it is the one term that rewards safety spending directly. It follows your record wherever you buy the policy, so it is worth knowing your factor and what is driving it, especially before comparing carriers.

Term 6: credits, programs, and safety groups

After the multiplication, programs subtract. Carriers file various credits, and New York has one program worth naming because its numbers are published: NYSIF safety groups. These are trade-based group plans with published upfront discounts averaging 32.5%, plus historical annual dividends that have averaged around 30%. Two honest qualifiers travel with that sentence, always: dividends are never assured, they are declared each year from the group's actual results, and admission is not automatic. The group manager has to accept the shop and NYSIF underwriting has to approve it.

For eligible Long Island trades this is often the largest single lever in the whole formula, which is why we wrote it up separately in our guide to NYSIF safety groups for Long Island shops.

What this means for your renewal

Three of the six terms deserve a look at every renewal, and this year a fourth joins them. The class code should match what the crew does now. The payroll estimate should match this year, not the biggest year on file. The mod should be known in advance, not a surprise. And because the published loss costs drop on October 1, 2026, the baseline under every carrier's multiplier moves, which means a multiplier that looked ordinary last year may not be ordinary now. A renewal on or after that date is the natural moment to compare, and the checklist post walks through the timing in detail.

One timing note for brand-new businesses: a new employer with no prior workers comp history can e-apply, e-sign, and pay a premium deposit electronically, and coverage can take effect at 12:01 a.m. the following day if the carrier approves. An established shop switching carriers typically waits 5 to 25 days, mostly on loss runs (typically five years) from the incumbent carrier. The full new-employer path is in workers comp for new contractors.

Why nobody honest quotes you a number without an application

Look back at the six terms. Two of them, payroll and claims history, exist only inside your shop, and one more, the class code, requires knowing your operations in detail. A figure produced without those inputs is a guess wearing a suit. That is why we never state a premium without a completed application, and why you should treat any number offered before an application, from anyone, as marketing rather than pricing.

What can be done honestly before an application is exactly what this article did: show the published terms, cite the filings, and point at which levers most shops in a class have available. The application, a ten-field form you review and e-sign yourself at the application page, is what turns published components into your actual figure, and the decision on it always belongs to the carrier.

The honest caveat. Everything above is from published filings, cited where each figure appears, and none of it is your number. We work from public data, not from your policy, which we have not seen, so every statement here is probability language about shops in a class, never a claim about your policy. Premiums are always paid to the carrier directly, never to us, and the carrier makes every coverage decision.
